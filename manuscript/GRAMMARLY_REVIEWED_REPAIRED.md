## Abstract

Background: Over the years, researchers have found that a prior Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection is present in almost all multiple sclerosis patients, with EBV seroconversion followed by an increase in serological neurofilament light chain, a biomarker of neuroaxonal injury. Molecular mimicry between viral and self-antigens is one proposed mechanism for this association. Results: In this study, we used a multi-stage computational pipeline incorporating NetMHCIIpan, MixMHC2pred, TCR-facing BLOSUM62 similarity scoring, and 3D structural modeling to classify candidate EBV human peptide pairs for future experimental peptide-HLA binding and register validation. Following an initial rigorous TCR-BLOSUM62 analysis of TCR-contact residues of 6,400 allele-specific pairs across four HLA class II alleles (HLA-DRB1*03:01, HLA-DRB1*08:01, HLA-DRB1*13:03, and HLA-DRB1*15:01), a targeted evidence-review panel of eight pairs underwent further binding affinity and register analysis, yielding two medium-priority pairs and six holds classified as such due to criteria gaps, including predictor discrepancies and register disagreements. In the eight-peptide evidence-review panel, the viral BALF5 (DNA polymerase catalytic subunit) peptide from 627-641 AA paired with the human TALDO1₂₁₆-₂₃₀ (DRB1*15:01) and TALDO1₁₀₈-₁₂₂ (DRB1*13:03) (transaldolase) peptides at rank 14/1,600 in HLA-DRB1*15:01 and rank 13/1,600 in HLA-DRB1*13:03. When the initial screen was expanded to a 49-pair evaluation to address the selectivity concerns, both BALF5-TALDO1 pairs alone were re-recovered, with BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-TALDO1₂₁₆-₂₃₀ in HLA-DRB1*15:01 being classified by the pipeline as a strong-medium candidate, supported by computational consensus and IEDB-listed BALF5 binding evidence. Structural modeling revealed binding register instability, while several evidence reviews found key gaps in public self-antigen presentation records, especially in alleles outside of the HLA-DR15 system. Conclusions: The study results provide a prioritized shortlist for experimental binding, register-mapping, and T-cell cross-reactivity testing.

## 1 Background

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease that can cause degeneration of motor, sensory, visual, and cognitive abilities through immune-mediated damage affecting myelin sheaths in the brain, spinal cord, and optic nerves [2]. In 2022, Bjornevik et al. studied a cohort of more than 10 million U.S. military personnel, 955 of whom developed MS. Their analysis reported an approximately 32-fold increase in MS risk following EBV infection; cytomegalovirus showed no comparable association. Serum neurofilament light chain increased only after EBV infection, indicating that EBV may precede detectable neurological injury [1]. Lanz et al. identified an antibody from an MS patient that recognized both EBV EBNA1 and the human CNS protein GlialCAM [3]. Previous studies, such as Lanz et al., identified cross-reactive antibodies [3]; comprehensive mapping of T cell cross-reactivity remains incompletely defined. T-cell receptors (TCRs) recognize a peptide presented in the binding groove of a human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecule rather than the peptide alone. Sequence-only peptide comparisons therefore do not define the HLA-binding component of immune recognition. Different HLA alleles also show distinct structural preferences, affecting pMHC binding affinity and the binding register— the placement of the nine-amino-acid core within the HLA groove. The register determines which residues are anchors and which are potential TCR contacts [20, 21]. The computational pipeline developed here integrates peptide-HLA binding predictions, register-aware sequence alignments, and predicted peptide-HLA structures to prioritize a small subset of viral-self peptide pairs before laboratory testing. Predicted sequence or structural resemblance does not establish stable cell-surface presentation, functional T-cell activation in vivo, or experimentally confirmed registers.

## 2 Materials and Methods

## 2.1 Study design

This study describes a computational prioritization pipeline designed to identify high-interest EBV-human self peptide candidate pairs for subsequent experimental peptide-HLA binding and binding-register validation. The workflow integrated predicted HLA-binding affinities, register-aware sequence alignments, structural modeling, and downstream evidence review. Computational predictions alone do not establish natural processing and presentation in vivo, surface pMHC stability, or TCR recognition and functional cross-reactivity.

## 2.2 Peptide curation and allele-specific pairing

An EBV repertoire was curated from the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) by filtering EBV source peptides with positive T-cell assay outcomes from MHC class II alleles in human hosts [4]. Human self-proteins included myelin-associated and CNS proteins from positive IEDB MHC-II T-cell records and additional 15-mer peptides derived from UniProt sequences [5]. Duplicate assay records sharing accession, coordinates, and sequence were merged. Imported IEDB records lacking an “Exact Epitope” classification or outside 11-30 amino acids were removed, as were sequences containing X, B, or Z. Forty EBV peptides and forty human peptides per allele, selected under evidence and protein-coverage rules, were paired within each allele to create 6,400 allele-specific comparisons. The initial sequence screen was limited to HLA-DRB1*03:01, HLA-DRB1*08:01, HLA-DRB1*13:03, and HLA-DRB1*15:01. Each HLA allele was evaluated independently; pMHCs from different alleles were not compared. HLA-DRA*01:01 was the alpha chain in every pMHC model.

## 2.3 TCR-facing sequence-similarity scoring

Sequence similarity was calculated with BLOSUM62 [8]. Because all non-peptide components are identical within an allele-bound comparison, scoring was restricted to predicted TCR-contact positions P2, P3, P5, P7, and P8 in aligned nine-residue binding cores. Higher scores indicated greater physicochemical resemblance and amino-acid substitution similarity at the potential TCR interface. Scores are relative sequence-prioritization measures, not measures of T-cell recognition, cross-reactivity, presentation, or molecular mimicry. Percentile rankings were calculated independently within each allele’s 1,600-pair cohort.

## 2.4 pMHC structural modeling

Heterotrimeric pMHC complexes (HLA-DRA + relevant HLA-DR β chain + target peptide) were modeled using the AlphaFold 3 web server across five returned models per job [9]. Quality checks verified coordinate completion. Reported metrics, including peptide pLDDT and peptide-chain ipTM, were used separately because well-defined HLA chains can dominate global complex confidence. The first 85 Cα atoms from HLA chain A and the first 85 Cα atoms from HLA chain B were aligned by Kabsch fitting. Structural deviation between peptides was quantified using backbone root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), measured over the shared 11-mer peptide window that was nested around the proposed nine-residue core. To prevent overstating the bounds of our claims, AlphaFold outputs were treated strictly as predicted models rather than experimental coordinates and analyzed independently of TCR docking simulations.

## 2.5 Evidence audit and candidate prioritization

Eight candidate pairs were selected from the 6,400-comparison allele-specific pairs for deeper review and underwent a standardized manual evidence audit. In this audit, the predicted binding support, predicted register agreement, IEDB sequence/provenance records, public immunopeptidome evidence, viral sequence conservation, and human self-protein tissue expression/rarity were evaluated. Based on this multi-layered evidence review, each pair was categorized as either high priority, medium priority, or hold. The pipeline placed no candidates as high-priority, two candidates as medium-priority, and six candidates on hold due to the failure of one or both peptide arms to meet the binding criteria, unverified provenance, missing support in immunopeptidomic presentation datasets, or register discrepancies. The two candidates selected as medium priority were BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-TALDO1₁₀₈-₁₂₂ for HLA-DRB1*13:03 and BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-TALDO1₂₁₆-₂₃₀ for HLA-DRB1*15:01. The results of the initial 8-pair evidence review were separate from the later 49-pair provisional classifications.

## 2.6 Binding predictions and binding-register analysis

For the initial eight-pair review, the pipeline predicted percentile ranks of peptide-HLA class II using NetMHCIIpan-4.3 and MixMHC2pred [6,7]. In order to achieve full binding consensus by the pipeline, a pair needed both predictors to have ≤5% for both the viral and self peptide arms. To achieve lesser binding support, at least one predictor needed to be ≤5%, or both predictors returned percentile ranks ≤20%. To maintain the claim boundary, peptides were marked as computationally predicted binders unless the IEDB data specified exact-match experimental binding assay records for the matching HLA allele. For this initial eight-pair review, the binding-register alignment of all peptide arms was analyzed. The binding register is defined as the predicted nine-residue P1-P9 core positioned within the open-ended HLA class II binding groove [20]. By using the core assignments determined by the NetMHCIIpan-4.3 EL and MixMHC2pred context algorithms, the pipeline assigned a predicted binding register and compared these registers against each other. Disagreements were defined as any different predicted core sequence (i.e., a shifted or overlapping nine-residue window) between the independent prediction algorithms, and were logged as a predicted register agreement/disagreement for each peptide. Then, the binding register was divided into major anchor residues (P1, P4, P6, P9), which were separated from predicted TCR-facing positions (P2, P3, P5, P7, P8), which were isolated for predictive TCR contact analysis.

## 2.7 Calibration and exploratory modeling

This computational pipeline also used a method of literature-derived calibration. The published Hy.2E11 system BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-MBP₈₅-₉₉ [10] cross-reactive benchmark served as a reference for our structural-comparison system. This control was composed of two structures: HLA-DRA*01:01+HLA-DRB5*01:01 (BALF5 (PDB: 1H15)) and HLA-DRA*01:01+HLA-DRB1*15:01 (MBP (PDB:1BX2)). Since this control pair contained two distinct HLA-DR β chains, it was categorized as a DR15 haplotype-level control system rather than a single-allele comparison. Therefore, this control system does not evaluate same-allele similarity or validate either BALF5-TALDO1 pairs or establish biological mimicry. Poisson-Boltzmann/APBS pMHC surface-potential comparison was explored as a pMHC surface-resemblance descriptor [11], but the electrostatic score results were excluded from primary candidate ranking because the control gate of 30 required results yielded 15 failed results and 12 missing results. This result didn’t test or refute biological T-cell cross-reactivity. An AlphaFold 3 TCR-pMHC calibration against 1YMM and 2WBJ experimental-reference complexes was assessed with DockQ and CAPRI pose-recovery criteria [12, 13]. Since all ten models, which were derived from two five-model calibration jobs, received a score under the DockQ acceptable-quality boundary of 0.23 and both controls were defined by CAPRI as “Incorrect,” these outputs were excluded from candidate TCR ranking. Separately from the AlphaFold3 TCR-pMHC method described above, a method using TCRModel2 was attempted. In this method, TCRModel2 generated five-component TCR-pMHC models with HLA-α, HLA-β, peptide, TCR α, and TCR β chains [14]. The calibration references 1YMM, 1ZGL, and 2WBJ, each receiving five models. DockQ and CAPRI classification were used alongside TCR-placement, pMHC, TCR-fold, and whole-complex RMSD between each TCRModel2 model and its experimental reference structure. 1ZGL had five acceptable models (DockQ of 0.411-0.479), 1YMM had five incorrect models (DockQ of 0.010-0.140), and 2WBJ had five incorrect models (DockQ of 0.016-0.038). Only one control met the recovery criterion. Candidate outputs were excluded from ranking and cannot support shared-TCR or cross-reactivity claims. Therefore, the BALF5 replicate models remain exploratory/internal predictions, not docking validations.

## 2.8 Reproducibility

Throughout the pipeline, a high degree of reproducibility was maintained through thorough documentation of the entire process found in our GitHub repository. In the GitHub repository, the software environments, random seeds, input logs, and dataset checksums (SHA-256) are all retained. Furthermore, the ranking algorithms were completely deterministic. For example, ties in sequence similarity were systematically broken using an order of physicochemical mismatch in the ascending direction, identity in the descending direction, and local-surface percentile, in the ascending direction. Additionally, for unevaluated or incomplete data in public databases, those data points were marked as “Not Reported” rather than negative. Finally, no nominal p-values, FDR correction, or probabilistic “mimicry probability” were used in the computational rankings. **Figure 1. ## 3.3 Focused Structural Assessment of Predicted Binding Registers

The two medium-priority pairs were structurally assessed alongside a DRB5*01:01 restriction test. Thirteen AlphaFold 3 jobs, each returning five models, generated 65 structural models. All passed checks for coordinate completeness, correct chain identifiers, and peptide-sequence integrity. In HLA-DRB1*13:03, viral parent-nested median RMSD was 0.445 Å; BALF5 showed consistent coordinate recovery, whereas TALDO1₁₀₈-₁₂₂ had lower confidence and positional uncertainty. In HLA-DRB1*15:01, viral and human RMSD values were 1.712 Å and 0.888 Å, respectively; viral BALF5 parent models varied, while the TALDO1₂₁₆-₂₃₀ 11-mer was comparatively consistent. In HLA-DRB5*01:01, viral RMSD was 0.247 Å and longer parent-peptide modeling did not stabilize either competing TALDO1 register. Models of shorter 11-mers frequently converged to consistent placements, but these placements were rarely retained when parent 15-mers were modeled, producing alternate poses and register shifts (Figure 4). ## 3.4 Cross-Method Structural Reproducibility

For the next stage of the computational model, cross-method structural reproducibility was implemented using PANDORA’s structural modelling tool coupled with MODELLER. For the first step, the PANDORA model was tested on its ability to model controls, passing its predefined benchmark of 120 models being scored via expected-register recovery, adjacent-decoy comparison, sequence QC, and template-leakage checks, with the two reciprocal targets of 1BX2 and 6CQQ being entered as controls. Both controls passed this gate, with each control having at least one top-five model with the expected register having a core-backbone RMSD below 2 Å. Both controls also had a lower top-five median RMSD than that of adjacent register decoys. Furthermore, both controls passed the exact-sequence and template-leakage tests. Subsequently, structural modeling was expanded to an ensemble of 1,200 models spanning 10 peptides (BALF5, TALDO1, and eight P6/P7 charge-substitution variants), evaluated across two structural templates and three register placements (20 models per condition). Then, all 10 predefined peptides were evaluated, and 0 of the 10 peptides (0/10) demonstrated support for the binding register that was expected (according to NetMHCIIpan and MixMHC2pred) across both structural templates. In another attempt at cross-method structural reproducibility, a subsequent AlphaFold structural modeling dataset was used. The modeling dataset yielded 150 completed models with 30 completed jobs of 5 returned models per job, with seven charge-substitution comparisons having uncertain binding-register placement and one comparison being considered as not-evaluable due to a seed mismatch. The implication of this finding is that the principal factor preventing meaningful structural comparison between the registers could be due to conformational instability and register drift within the proposed core windows across templates and replicates. This shows the register instability involved in structural modeling tools like AlphaFold3 and Pandora/MODELLER for BALF5/TALDO1 charge-substitution inputs and therefore the results of binding register indication should not be interpreted as evidence that charged substitutions disrupted the binding in this context.

## 3.5 Expanded Assessment of 49 Candidate Pairs

After the initial screening was determined to be selective, the computational pipeline created a separate, expanded cohort. These 49 candidate pairs were recovered from the original 6,400 allele-specific pair list in the sequence similarity ranking. We then completed computational binding affinity and core-register prediction using NetMHCIIpan, MixMHC2pred, and IEDB-recommended binding for all 98 peptides. To ensure consistency in the data, the registers evaluated by NetMHCIIpan were compared against registers determined by MixMHC2pred and considered alongside IEDB recommended binding. Across 49 peptide pairs, 44 pairs had at least two predictors selecting the same declared 9-mer core for both peptide arms, while two of the peptide pairs had all three predictor percentile ranks ≤20% for both peptide arms. This demonstrates the high degree of computational register-predictor agreement between the predictive models, rather than any experimentally confirmed structural placement within the HLA groove. BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-TALDO1₁₀₈-₁₂₂ in HLA-DRB1*13:03 and BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-TALDO1₂₁₆-₂₃₀ in HLA-DRB1*15:01 were the only pairs to satisfy the combined binding and register criteria. In an attempt to ground the computational data with experimental data, 98 automated queries were attempted through the IEDB API, of which 0 retrievable public-evidence records were returned because the API endpoint was unavailable, which led to the classification as not evaluable. Therefore, no candidates were classified as high-priority. Later, targeted manual verification within the IEDB recovered a direct, experimentally confirmed positive binding record for the viral BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁ peptide in the context of HLA-DRB1*15:01. However, the recovered assay only confirmed the viral peptide and didn’t confirm the self-peptide (TALDO1₂₁₆-₂₃₀) binding, stable presentation, or dual-reactive T cell-recognition.

## 3.6 Secondary Provisional Classifications

After analysis that utilized sequence metrics and targeted assay verification, a provisional shortlist was created, consisting of seven prioritized candidates. Of the seven prioritized candidates, one was described as a strong-medium candidate, two were described as weak-medium candidates, and four were described as strong-low candidates. The remaining forty-two candidates were left untiered due to insufficient evidence to justify a priority ranking, and detailed tier explanations are described later in this section. Moreover, no pairs were classified as high-priority due to the unavailability of a public evidence-retrieval endpoint in the API. These tier categories are completely distinct from the earlier 8-pair review.

## 4 Discussion

## 4.1 Principal Findings

In this study, a multi-stage computational screening and evidence review workflow was implemented to prioritize candidate EBV-human peptide pairs for later experimental validation. The central finding of the paper was the two BALF5-TALDO1 pairings (BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-TALDO1₂₁₆-₂₃₀ and BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-TALDO1₁₀₈-₁₂₂). Although the HLA-DRB1*15:01 BALF5-TALDO1 pair has the strongest support, the HLA-DRB1*13:03 BALF5-TALDO1 pair demonstrated weaker support primarily due to the lack of verified public presentation data under the review protocol. Alongside these two primary candidates, a small subset of secondary candidates was also identified, notably including the template-consistent EBNA1₈₇-₁₀₁-MBP₂₁₇-₂₃₃ pair in HLA-DRB1*15:01 alongside some provisionally strong-low candidates like EBNA2₁₃₆-₁₅₀-MOG₉₇-₁₀₉ and EBNA1₈₇-₁₀₁-ANO2₇₉-₉₃. The benefit of this pipeline was that, for future experiments, rather than claiming functional molecular mimicry in vivo, the defined computational results deliver an explicit, prioritized shortlist focused explicitly on guiding in vitro peptide-HLA binding affinity and structural register-mapping experiments. Although the BALF5-TALDO1 pairs may not have had the most convincing sequence similarity scores, like EBNA1₈₇-₁₀₁-ANO2₇₉-₉₃ and EBNA3B₁₃₉-₁₅₃-MOG₂₂₁-₂₃₅, the raw sequence alignment represents only an initial layer rather than the sole driver of molecular similarity. Another reason why the BALF5-TALDO1 pairs remained so prominent was because they were the only viral-self pairings across the entire 49-pair cohort to satisfy the full binding consensus criteria alongside dual-peptide register agreement in both HLA-DRB1*13:03 and HLA-DRB1*15:01 contexts. Furthermore, in HLA-DRB1*15:01, the targeted database verification recovery of direct experimental binding evidence for the viral BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁ peptide demonstrates a grounding that most other epitopes didn’t have. That said, the self-antigen peptide (TALDO1₂₁₆-₂₃₀) remains unconfirmed by any ligand or direct binding assays. On the other hand, in HLA-DRB1*13:03, even though both peptides in the pair met the predictive thresholds, the pair was downgraded due to the complete absence of allele-specific public binding records found under our review protocol. Finally, the replacement of the initial review tiers with the expanded provisional classification reflected the decrease in reliance on conflicting scores and increasing the reliance on several defined factors like structural template stability, and verified public assay data.

## 4.3 Contribution of Structural Analyses

In this computational pipeline, there was a mix in the use of predictive algorithms and structural comparison. This is because a high consensus among sequence-based register prediction algorithms like NetMHCIIpan or MixMHC2Pred doesn’t guarantee structural coordinate stability and therefore, structural analysis of 3D models generated by AlphaFold was necessary. Furthermore, sequence analyses often were biased at shorter lengths. Specifically, through the analysis, it was observed that shorter 11-mer nested core peptides modeled into stable conformations in the models analyzed, but those exact conformations were often lost or shifted when the 11-mer’s parent 15-mer peptide was used. Additionally, structural analyses were also very important in isolating the local confidence metrics (i.e., peptide-local pLDDT and peptide-chain ipTM), which were important in the evaluation of the modeled structure, as global confidence metrics often obscure peptide uncertainty due to high heterodimer confidence scores. Structural analyses were also impactful in predicting the reason for some discrepancies. For example, the comparison between the AlphaFold3 and PANDORA structures revealed that the conformational instability could be a result of the tested modelling conditions rather than a biophysical issue. Finally, another area where the structural modeling was important was the contrast between template retention. For example, while the BALF5-TALDO1 pair showed unresolved structural register drift across models at times, the EBNA1₈₇-₁₀₁-MBP₂₁₇-₂₃₃ pair stably maintained its declared register across the calibrated PANDORA templates, but structural analyses proved to be useful in determining how this computational reproducibility was a result of geometric plausibility rather than proof of native binding.

## 4.4 Linkage to prior research

While large-scale cohort studies like the one conducted by Bjornevik et al. established the epidemiological link between EBV infection and multiple sclerosis and landmark structural studies like the one conducted by Lanz et al., which supported high-affinity antibody cross-reactivity between EBNA1 and GlialCAM, the mechanisms described in these papers operate under fundamentally different molecular rules than pMHC class II T-cell recognition. Furthermore, following the literature definition of T-cell cross-reactivity, which defines cross-reactivity as the dual recognition of peptide-HLA complexes, this project’s claims were limited to computational candidate prioritization and predicted peptide-HLA similarities. Several pairs that seem familiar to the literature of molecular mimicry between EBV and MS proteins like EBNA1₈₇-₁₀₁-ANO2₇₉-₉₃ [18] and EBNA1₈₇-₁₀₁-MBP₂₁₇-₂₃₃ emerged within the sequence-based and structure-based screens. That said, shared protein pairings must be distinguished from the exact epitopes. Finally, the use of high-scoring literature-informed pairings as independent computational validation was avoided, as these antigens were deliberately selected based on literature-grounded disease associations because literature-based recovery can inflate apparent recovery.

## 4.5 Additional Exploratory Methods

While Poisson-Boltzmann electrostatic surface potential comparison was explored, it was excluded from primary candidate ranking because the electrostatic similarity scoring failed to discriminate between the positive calibration control benchmark and background decoys. Additionally, TCRModel2 docking simulations were attempted to evaluate pMHC-TCR complexes. Since the pose recovery didn’t consistently meet the DockQ acceptable binding score of 0.23, meeting the boundary for the experimental structure 1YMM but not 2WBJ or 1BX2. Therefore, docking outputs could not be utilized to confirm candidate-specific TCR recognition. Importantly, these failures of exploratory modeling tools reflect current algorithmic constraints rather than biophysical proof that cross-reactivity is absent in vivo.

## 4.6 Limitations

The search space was limited to pre-curated viral epitopes, selected myelin/CNS proteins, and four HLA-DRB1 alleles. HLA-DQ and HLA-DP loci were omitted. Rankings shifted under TCR-facing BLOSUM62, full-core BLOSUM62, and strict physicochemical-mismatch formulations. Predictors may share training data, potentially limiting the independence of their agreement. The unavailable public-evidence endpoint and absent self-peptide records did not establish biological absence of presentation. In silico modeling also cannot account for endogenous antigen processing, HLA-DM-mediated peptide editing, pMHC stability, antigen-presenting-cell context, or downstream TCR-signaling thresholds [22].

## 4.7 Experimental Next Steps

Peptide-HLA binding can be quantified for viral and self peptides against recombinant HLA-DRB1 molecules by fluorescence polarization or surface plasmon resonance. Register mapping may use alanine-scanning mutagenesis and structural methods such as X-ray crystallography or cryo-EM. Matched T cells should be tested against both peptide-HLA complexes for dual functional activation. Endogenous processing can be assessed through peptide elution and mass spectrometry from infected or inflamed antigen-presenting cells.

## 5 Conclusions

The first experimental priority is HLA-DRB1*15:01 BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-TALDO1₂₁₆-₂₃₀, because of its strong medium classification, its passing of expanded binding-and-register criteria, and exact allele-specific viral-arm IEDB binding evidence [4]. Recombinant peptide-HLA binding should test viral and self arms separately, followed by register mapping because structural analyses did not identify a single stable backbone register. The next candidate is HLA-DRB1*13:03 BALF5₆₂₇-₆₄₁-TALDO1₁₀₈-₁₂₂; both arms passed the expanded computational gate, while absent public evidence was the major factor limiting its tier. The third priority is HLA-DRB1*15:01 EBNA1₈₇-₁₀₁-MBP₂₁₇-₂₃₃, whose predicted register persisted across calibrated PANDORA template analysis despite failure of full binding consensus. The strong-low candidates should follow once binding and register outcomes for these top three are available. Natural presentation should then be tested by immunopeptidomics, followed by matched-TCR assays against both pMHC complexes and functional-activation measurements; docking similarity alone is insufficient.
